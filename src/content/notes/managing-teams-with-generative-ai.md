A manager deciding how a team should use AI faces several connected questions. Which work can be delegated? What counts as a good result? Where should the saved time go? How will people learn to judge work they no longer produce entirely themselves?

Those questions offer a more useful starting point than a prediction about whether managers will disappear. Different teams have different tasks, constraints, and consequences of error. A change in how one task is performed does not establish how many people a department needs.

**This is source-backed analysis and a proposed management framework.** The findings below are reported by their original authors. Model Fieldnotes has not reproduced them, and the examples are illustrative.

## What the evidence says

**Survey evidence.** A [March 2026 Gartner release](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2026-3-4-gartner-hr-survey-reveals-45-percent-of-managers-report-ai-has-lived-up-to-their-expectations) describes a July 2025 survey of 1,973 managers in which only 14% reported no challenges driving effective AI use across their teams. A separate survey of 114 HR leaders found that 7% of organizations provided guidance on using time saved by AI. These are respondent reports, not experimental measurements of productivity or proof that a particular management practice works.

**Workplace research.** In the published 2025 [Generative AI at Work study](https://danielle-li.github.io/assets/docs/GenerativeAIatWork.pdf), Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond analyze a staggered rollout involving 5,172 customer-support workers. They report a 15% average increase in issues resolved per hour, with larger gains among less experienced workers. The strongest workers saw small speed gains and small quality declines. The setting and rollout design matter: this is not a randomized test of all knowledge work, and its average is not a forecast for another company.

**Analyst interpretation.** Gartner’s [May 2025 public engineering-leadership Q&A](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-05-08-generative-ai-is-redefining-the-role-of-software-engineering-leaders) emphasizes skills development and clearer responsibility across engineering and data teams. Treat this as professional guidance; its forward-looking claims are forecasts.

Together, these sources motivate questions about adoption and differentiated support. They do not establish a single new job description for every manager.

## Make delegation an explicit agreement

For an illustrative reporting team, AI might gather approved inputs and assemble a draft. A domain expert checks meaning and exceptions; an authorized person releases the result. The manager’s work includes making this division understandable and ensuring review has an owner.

Before delegating a task, agree on four things: the intended outcome, the actions permitted, the evidence required for acceptance, and the point at which a person takes over. A vague instruction to “use AI more” does not answer any of them.

Write the agreement in language the team uses. “Prepare a draft using these sources; flag missing data; do not send it” is easier to assess than a broad productivity aspiration. Technical permissions should enforce the action boundary independently of the prompt. The [constraints-through-compaction guide](/fieldbook/forgotten-instruction/) makes that distinction visible.

## Coach judgment as well as tool use

An AI-assisted output can conceal what its author understood. A useful coaching conversation asks why a recommendation fits the situation, which inputs it depends on, and how the person checked it.

Use a sample of real work to discuss both accepted and rejected suggestions. Let newer team members explain their checks and invite experienced colleagues to surface exceptions. Preserve opportunities to practice the underlying skill when being able to perform it remains part of the role.

This is a proposed learning practice, not a measured consequence of the cited studies. Its value should be evaluated through the quality of decisions and a person’s ability to explain or correct the work—not simply through how frequently they open an AI tool.

## Decide where time saved should go

Treat reclaimed time as a choice to discuss. It could support faster service, deeper customer research, fewer recurring errors, skill development, or a more sustainable workload. Each choice needs a corresponding outcome to check.

For example, a team that reduces the time spent drafting updates might invest that capacity in resolving a recurring source of customer confusion. The expected benefit would then be fewer repeat questions or better resolution, rather than a larger volume of updates. This example is a hypothesis, not a reported result.

Make room for employees to challenge estimates of time saved. Preparing inputs, checking answers, and correcting mistakes are real work. A manager needs to know whether effort disappeared, moved to another person, or shifted to a later stage.

## Keep trust and accountability visible

A useful team agreement explains how AI use will be discussed, what data may be used, and how mistakes can be reported. Ask employees where the tool creates extra effort or makes them uncertain about the expectations placed on them.

Keep responsibility attached to people and roles. “The AI suggested it” does not explain who authorized an action or checked the result. Equally, a person assigned to review needs sufficient context and time to do so. An approval step that cannot be performed meaningfully creates a misleading sense of control.

## A conversation to have with your team

Use these prompts to examine one workflow before expanding it:

| Question                                 | An answer you can inspect                                    |
| ---------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| What are we trying to improve?           | A named user outcome and a baseline                          |
| What will AI do, and where will it stop? | A written delegation boundary                                |
| Who checks the result?                   | A named role, acceptance criteria, and time to review        |
| What changes for people?                 | Training needs, review effort, and a place to raise concerns |
| How will we use any freed capacity?      | An agreed priority and a way to assess its benefit           |

There is no evidence here that these questions eliminate management challenges. They make the decisions explicit enough to test and revise.
